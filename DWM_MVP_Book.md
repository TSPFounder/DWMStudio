# The Mountain's Turbine

*A story of the Dream World Maker*

---

## Chapter One — The Mountain

The turbine had been standing on the ridge above the village for as long as anyone had lived there, and in all that time it had never turned.

It was not broken, exactly. Nobody had crashed into it or torn anything off it. It had simply come with the land, the way the rocks had come with the land, and the people who settled there had arrived without the paperwork and without anyone who knew what the paperwork would have said. The blades hung still against the sky. In summer the shadow of them moved across the top pasture the way the shadow of a dead tree does, going nowhere.

Hank stood looking up at it, which he did most mornings, and which everyone in the village had long ago stopped remarking on.

"That turbine came with the land when we settled here," he said. "Nobody's turned it in years, and nobody left so much as a drawing of how it goes back together. We need real plans before anyone touches a wrench, and hands, parts, and food for the crew once we do."

He turned to the apprentice.

"Head down to the other communities. Trade fair, come back with what we need, and let's get this thing spinning again."

It was not a small request, and he did not pretend it was. The Mountain had two things worth trading — timber off the slopes, and, when the turbine finally turned, power. Everything else had to come up the road. Everything on that mountain had come up the road at some point, carried the last mile by somebody's back.

*What exactly do we need?*

"Start with Hillside — they've got engineers who can put together real CAD drawings and a simulation model, so whoever fixes this thing isn't guessing. After that: Grain, to feed the crew while they're working the mount. Hands that know rigging, because none of us have hung something this heavy before. And tools from the city — precision work no one up here can forge."

Four things, from four different places, and none of them from here.

"Out through the gate and follow the track down to Hillside. Ask for Sophia Sandoval — she's got the room above the realty office. She'll be expecting you."

The village behind him was fuller than it had been the year before. The labour hall crew had come up from the Suburb and finished a row of new houses the week previous — DeShawn's people doing the building, Hillside's lumber doing the standing up — because the realty office had found three families and had nowhere to put them. It was crowded now. Nobody was sleeping in a barn.

Up on the top ground, Hillside's sheep were grazing out the summer. Nothing else that ground was good for, and it was good for that.

Solar panels on the workshop roof held the place over while the turbine stood dead. They ran the lights and the radio and not much else.

## Chapter Two — Hillside

The road down came out at the top of a market street, and the realty office sat where the street began, with the same sign over the door that every realty office in every community had over its door. Above it, up a flight of stairs, three people worked in one room with drawings pinned across the wall.

Sophia Sandoval looked at what the apprentice had come about and laughed, not unkindly.

"So that's the turbine that came with your land. Ambitious purchase — nobody's touched that thing in years." She gestured at the two behind her. "Good news is, my two here already worked up what you need to bring it back."

*What exactly did you put together?*

"Full CAD drawings of the mount and rotor assembly, plus a Simulink model of how it should actually behave once it's running. Owen did the drawings, Nathan built the model. Between the two, whoever's doing the repair up there won't be guessing."

Owen Marsh had done the harder half and knew it.

"I pulled the mount and rotor assembly apart in CAD, piece by piece," he said. "Whoever repairs that thing won't have to reverse-engineer it in the field — every bracket, every bolt pattern, it's all there." He shrugged. "Hardest part wasn't the rotor — it was the mount. Whoever built that turbine originally didn't leave much documentation. Had to measure half of it by hand from photos."

He added, and meant it:

"If the repair crew finds something in the field that doesn't match my drawings, tell them to trust what they're looking at, not the paper. Old hardware doesn't always match what was on file."

Nathan Ferris had built the other half.

"Owen's drawings tell you what the turbine looks like. My model tells you how it's supposed to behave once it's spinning again — load, response, where it'll struggle. Saves you finding out the hard way." He was honest about its limits without being apologetic. "Model's only as good as what we know about that specific turbine. I built it off Owen's CAD data and some reasonable assumptions — real sensor data once it's running would tighten it up, but it'll get you started. Half my job is knowing when the simple model is good enough and when it isn't. For a first repair pass, simple's fine."

The trade went on the ledger. Stone moved: Mountain's balance down, Hillside's up, the two changes exactly equal and opposite, the way they always were.

"Here's everything — drawings and model both," Sophia said. "Take care of it; that's real engineering hours in your hands, not just a sketch on a napkin."

Then she said the thing that would take four more stops to make sense of.

"One more thing, and you won't like it. Doing this properly — not patching it, properly — the design hours alone are steep. Whether that's worth paying depends on what the parts cost, and that's not my number to give you. Ask Mike in the City. Put his figure next to mine before you decide anything."

Through the window behind her the mill was running. You could hear it when the wind sat right.

"Some people would call that noise," she said. "I'd rather hear it going than not."

Half of what came off that saw was no longer logs. The Suburb sent up salvage — old framing, pallets, whatever had been pulled out of a building — and Hillside pressed it back into board and beam. It took more sorting than milling did. The nails were the worst of it.

"Most of it goes back into houses. Realty office finds people somewhere to live and then somebody has to actually build the somewhere — that's been us for a while now. Half the new places in three towns came off that saw."

She said one more thing about the beams, in the tone of someone repeating a fact she found pleasing.

"Funny thing about a pressed beam — it's stronger than the tree it came out of, and steadier with it. A sawn board is only ever as good as its worst knot. Break the wood up and bond it back together and the flaws are all still in there. They've just stopped being in the same place."

There was one thing they still bought.

"Glue's the part nobody thinks about. It's the one thing in that press we buy in — and we buy it in barrels." She frowned at the window. "Bark, though. We strip tons of it off the logs and it's done nothing for us but pile up. Turns out you can cook a binder out of it. Maria reckons she can grow the other half of what we'd need. Between the two we'd be most of the way there."

"Most of the way, mind. There's a hardener in it we haven't a hope of making, and there's no talking a beam into holding without it."

**"Down the stairs, right as you come out of the office, then straight on down the market street — don't turn off it. Road climbs out the far end and drops you into the Valley. Maria Vega'll be on her porch. She generally is."**

"Good luck up there. Come back through when it's spinning — I'd like to see it."

## Chapter Three — The Valley

The road climbed out of Hillside and dropped into flat country, and Maria Vega was on her porch, which is where she generally was.

She did not need the situation explained twice.

"Feeding a work crew on a mountain, in the cold, for however long it takes to hang a turbine? That's food money, not tool money." She considered it. "Good thing we had a strong season — grain's the bulk of it, but there's plenty else coming off this land too."

The ledger moved again.

"Here — grain'll keep your crew fed a good while on its own. Ledger says it's fair, and I trust the ledger."

She talked while the carts were loaded, in the manner of someone who has had the same conversation with weather and machinery for thirty years and finds people an easier audience.

"Grain's what moves on the ledger, but it's not all we grow. Vegetables, some meat, honey from the hives out past the east field — none of it's tracked the way the grain is, but nobody up here goes hungry either."

The silo had a solar panel on it now, and a battery, and a small grey control box that kept the whole arrangement from destroying itself.

"That control box on the silo — Hillside drew it, City built it, and DeShawn's people put it in. Three towns for one little grey box." She seemed to enjoy this. "Nobody thought that was strange, which is the part I like."

Nothing organic left the valley. Stalks, husks, whatever the animals produced — all of it went back on the fields or into the digester.

"Everybody's got their own way of not wasting things. Ours just smells worse than most."

The tallest structure the Valley had ever built stood over the houses holding nothing but water.

"Sits high enough to push a line up onto a roof without a pump — no motor to seize, nothing to switch on. Just weight and gravity, which is the only thing round here that never stopped working."

She looked at it a moment longer than she needed to.

"We don't run it often, mind. Wet thatch rots, and a roof we soak for practice is a roof we're replacing early. So we keep it full, keep it from freezing, and we don't truly know it works."

A pause.

"Which is a thing I try not to dwell on of an evening."

After harvest, Hillside's sheep came down to eat the stubble off, and the Valley folded them on the fields at night.

"That's the whole point of it. Ground's better in spring for having had them on it, and nobody bought a sack of anything to make that happen." She was careful about the ownership. "Their flock, mind. Their wool. We just get the winter of them, and the good it does the soil. Suits everybody, which is rarer than it sounds."

There was talk, she said, of the Valley growing thatching straw — not the wheat they grew now, because the combine chewed the stalk to bits and you needed it long and whole. A different seed, cut with a binder, threshed gentle. Slower, and less grain off the same ground.

"But a thatched roof keeps a house warmer than anything anybody round here can buy, and it costs nobody a dollar. Suburb's got the hands that know how to lay it. So it's not whether — it's what we give up growing to do it."

**"Left out of the house and follow the dirt road down — it runs all the way into the Suburb. Ask for DeShawn Okafor; he works out of the realty office there. Walk straight in, he won't stand on ceremony."**

"Tell Hank the Valley says good luck. And tell him he still owes us from last winter."

## Chapter Four — The Suburb

The dirt road ran down out of the valley and into a place that had been built for more people than currently lived in it, and had adapted by using the surplus for other purposes.

DeShawn Okafor did not stand on ceremony.

"Rigging work on a mountain turbine? That's a real job, not a favor. We've got hands that know heavy lifting and know how to stay safe doing it — but good labor isn't free, and neither's the paperwork to send them up there."

The Stone side was simple.

"Stone covers the crew's time. Straightforward."

The other side was not.

"One more thing — insurance and permits for a job this size, that's not Stone. That comes out of the community vault, and it's not cheap." He looked up. "You sure Mountain can cover it?"

The vault was dollars, and dollars were the thing that only went one direction. Stone moved between communities and always summed to nothing; it could be created by the act of trading and destroyed by the act of settling, and no amount of it ever left. Dollars left. Dollars went out to whoever sold permits and insurance and never came back, and the number in the vault went down, and there was no mechanism anywhere in five communities for making it go up again except selling something outward.

The number went down.

"Paperwork's clear. Crew's yours."

Half the roofs on the block had panels, and the old garage held a power bank soaking up whatever they caught.

"Wasn't cheap to set up, but it beats waiting on somebody else's grid." He knocked the housing with a knuckle. "Funny thing about recycled parts — half that control station's housing used to be something else entirely. We don't waste much out here. Can't afford to."

Two streams left the Suburb and came back as other things.

"Anything we strip that's worth melting goes up to Mike in the City — comes back as castings we couldn't make ourselves. Fair trade. Neither of us has the whole shop, but between us it's most of one."

"Wood's the same story, different direction. Framing, pallets, anything we pull out of a building that isn't rotted — that goes up to Hillside and comes back as beam. We pull the nails here. They'd rather we did, and honestly so would their saw."

He mentioned, without being asked, that his crews ate Valley grain on every job that ran over a week, because Maria would not let him send anybody up a mountain on what the canteen called food.

And he mentioned the thing that kept him up.

"Five towns, one set of buses, and every drop of fuel in them bought with dollars we don't get back. That's the part that keeps me up." He rubbed his jaw. "Maria reckons the Valley could grow what we'd need instead, and converting the engines is shop work — we could do it here, or City could. Nobody's arguing about whether. Just about when."

**"City's too far to walk and I'm not sending you. Stop's on the corner — take the loop. Ask for Mike Dayton, he'll be on the shop floor, and he'll be the loudest thing in the building."**

"Tell your people to keep the ropes tied right. We'll see them when the job's done."

## Chapter Five — The City

The bus was old, and yellow, and had been built to carry children to a school that no longer existed. It carried five communities' worth of people now, between five communities, and it had a number painted on the side rather than a town's name on it, because it did not belong to a town.

Mike Dayton was the loudest thing in the building.

"Got Owen's CAD drawings from Hillside a few days back — went through them, worked up what it'll actually take to machine replacement parts for that mount. Good drawings, too. Made the estimate a lot less of a guess."

The parts, when they came off the bench, were exactly what had been drawn.

"This'll hold. Good steel, machined straight off Owen's drawings — not the kind of thing that fails halfway up a mountain."

Then he put down the invoice and said the second half of Sophia's sentence, four stops and several days later.

"Now — what you're paying me for is this batch. If you're asking what it costs to keep that machine running for good, every part on Owen's drawings has to be made one at a time, by us, forever. I can put a number on that." He wiped his hands. "You won't enjoy it, and it's only half the picture — Sophia's got the design side. Take both to Hank."

He reached onto a shelf behind him for a bearing, and handed it over.

"Here — that bearing's off our own shelf, not made for your mount. Same drawing though, so it'll drop straight in." He watched to see whether it landed. "That's the part that ought to interest you more than the invoice."

The furnace ran near enough around the clock. The feedstock for it was mostly Suburb scrap — DeShawn's crews stripped it, the City melted it. Cheaper than buying billet, and it kept two towns working.

What came off the furnace floor did not go to waste either.

"Scale off the furnace floor, rust off anything not worth saving — grind it fine enough and there's your red. Roast it hotter and it goes purple. Half the paint in five towns started as something we swept up."

He said the rest of it without any edge on it at all.

"Anything that isn't a red or an ochre or a black came from outside, and it came out of somebody's vault. That's not me telling anyone not to."

"You can just see it from the road, is all. Blue especially. There's no making blue — not here, not from anything we've got. So when you see a blue door you know exactly what it cost, and near enough what it meant to somebody."

**"You'll want Kai before you go anywhere. Straight up the stairs at the back — office over the shop floor. Metal's only half of what you're carrying home."**

Upstairs, above the noise, Kai Sutherland had the other half.

"Took Nathan's Simulink model and turned it into real controller code — C, running on the hardware, not just a diagram anymore. And since it needed a home, I laid out the controller enclosure myself in Fusion. Wind speed, load, the whole picture — that's on us. Software's not free, but neither is guessing wrong on wind speed."

The last trade went on the ledger.

"Done. Controller's built, code's flashed, it'll report in real-time once it's spinning. You'll know before it does if something's wrong."

The enclosure, he said, was nothing fancy — it only had to keep the electronics dry and let heat out without letting weather in. He had modelled it in an afternoon. The code had taken considerably longer than the box.

Half the boards in the district's control station had been pulled out of dead equipment and re-flowed. The Suburb sent up the carcasses; the City took the parts nobody else could reuse.

"Cheaper than new, and there isn't a 'new' to buy most weeks anyway."

**"Loop runs back round the way you came — stays on it all the way up to Mountain. Get on, and the next thing you'll be looking at is that turbine. Hank'll be waiting."**

"Good luck up there. I'll be watching the feed once it's live."

## Chapter Six — The Mountain Again

Hank was waiting, and everything was there.

Drawings from Hillside, so nobody had to guess at a bolt pattern eighty feet up. Grain from the Valley, so the crew could stay until the work was finished rather than until the food ran out. Hands from the Suburb who had hung heavy things before. Steel from the City, machined off the same drawings the crew were reading from.

"Real plans for the mount, grain for the crew, hands to do the rigging, and tools to finish it right. That's everything." Hank looked up at the ridge. "Let's bring this old thing back to life."

It took days. Nothing about it was dramatic. Ropes were tied correctly, because DeShawn had asked that they be, and somebody checked them, and somebody checked them again. At one point a bracket in the field did not match Owen's drawing and the crew trusted what they were looking at, exactly as they had been told to.

Then the brake came off, and the wind — which had been blowing across that ridge, unused, for the entire lifetime of everyone standing on it — got hold of the blades.

The rotor turned.

It turned slowly at first and then at the speed it had been designed for and had never once reached, and the number on Kai's feed came up green in a room ninety miles away, and for a while nobody on the mountain said anything at all.

## Chapter Seven — The Verdict

Hank let them have the afternoon before he said the rest of it.

"Something you should hear before you get comfortable. Sophia priced the engineering. Mike priced the parts."

He had put the two numbers next to each other, which was the one thing neither Sophia nor Mike could have done, because neither of them had the other's figure.

"Put those two together and this old machine costs more to keep alive than it gives back — not this winter, but soon enough."

The turbine was turning over his head while he said it.

*So the repair was a waste?*

"Opposite. It buys us the winter, and it bought us a straight answer, which we didn't have before."

He was not being kind about it. He was being accurate. The machine on the ridge was too old and too large and too particular; every part it would ever need would have to be made one at a time by a shop ninety miles away, forever, from drawings that had been reconstructed by hand from photographs. It would run. It would keep running for as long as they kept feeding it. And what it gave back would never quite cover what it took.

"Nobody up here could have worked that out alone — took their drawings and their shop floor both."

Which left the question of what to do, and Hank had already decided, and so, it turned out, had everybody else.

"What we do with it is build our own, smaller, and build the tools to make them."

Not one turbine. Not one community's turbine. Smaller machines, several of them, built to one set of drawings that everybody held a copy of — and, before any of that, the tooling to make the machines. A mill that pressed board flat enough to machine a mold from. A router to cut the mold. A foundry to cast what the router could not. A lathe that some of them would have to build before they could use it.

It would take years. The old turbine would carry them through the part of it that mattered.

That evening the blades were still going round, and the light off the ridge was the ordinary light of a machine doing what it was for, which nobody there had seen before.

"There it goes," Hank said. "Every community up on that ledger had a hand in this — yours included."

He was looking at the drawings, though, and not at the turbine.

"And now we've all got the same drawings. Next one, we build ourselves."

## Afterword — What the Ledger Said

Nothing in this story required anyone to be generous.

Every exchange in it went onto a ledger, at a price, and was settled. Hillside was paid for the drawings. The Valley was paid for the grain. The Suburb was paid for the crew, and charged the Mountain separately for the permits, in a currency that does not come back. The City was paid for the steel and the code.

Mutual credit works by a piece of arithmetic that is easy to state and takes a while to feel: when Mountain's balance goes down, someone else's goes up by exactly the same amount, and the total across all five communities is always zero. Nobody prints it. Nobody holds a reserve of it. It comes into being the moment two people agree on a price, and it disappears the moment the debt is settled, and the only thing it ever measures is who currently owes what to whom.

Which means the ledger cannot show a community that stands alone. There is no arrangement of those numbers in which one community is positive and no other is negative. The arithmetic will not permit it.

Nobody in the story says this. The people in it are busy — with a saw that needs sorting, with a tower they hope they never test, with a flock that has to be walked up a mountain in May and back down in October, with straw that has to be cut a slower way if it is going to keep a roof warm. They talk about nails and glue and paint and what it costs to make a mold twice.

But a small grey box on a silo in the Valley was drawn in one community, built in a second, and installed by a third, for one farm, and — as Maria said, and it is the closest anyone comes to saying it at all —

> *"Nobody thought that was strange, which is the part I like."*
